# Reasonable Expectation of Privacy

Edition label: `2026-10`

**Definition:** The two-prong Fourth Amendment test from Justice Harlan's *Katz* concurrence: a person enjoys constitutional protection where (1) they exhibit an actual subjective expectation of privacy, and (2) the expectation is one that society is prepared to recognize as reasonable.[^1]

## Overview

Articulated in Harlan's concurring opinion in *Katz v. United States* (1967), the two-prong reasonable-expectation inquiry added a privacy-based framework to Fourth Amendment doctrine.[^2] It did not displace the property/trespass test: *United States v. Jones* (2012) confirms that a physical trespass to obtain information remains an independent route to finding a search.[^3]

The test's structural ambiguity — what counts as a "reasonable" expectation, and whose perspective on "society" decides — has been a central interpretive question in later surveillance cases. *Smith v. Maryland* (1979) applied the third-party doctrine, first set out for bank records in *United States v. Miller* (1976), to dialed telephone numbers: information voluntarily disclosed to a third party (e.g., the phone company) carries no reasonable expectation of privacy,[^4] opening the constitutional space the post-9/11 Section 215 bulk-telephony program exploited.[^5] *United States v. Jones* (2012) confirmed that trespass remains an independent route,[^6] and *Carpenter v. United States* (2018) partially limited the third-party doctrine for cell-site location information.[^7]

## Analytical significance

The reasonable-expectation test matters to both judicial review and public oversight of classified surveillance. The Privacy and Civil Liberties Oversight Board (PCLOB) Section 215 report addresses the government's and the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA) court's view that under *Smith v. Maryland* there is no reasonable expectation of privacy in dialed numbers; the Wyden-Udall correspondence addressed the secret legal interpretation of Section 215 itself.[^14] The third-party doctrine — set out in *United States v. Miller* (1976) and applied to dialed numbers in *Smith v. Maryland* (1979) — is the doctrinal hinge.[^8]
