# Trespass Doctrine

Edition label: `2026-10`

**Definition:** The pre-*Katz* Fourth Amendment rule, established in *Olmstead v. United States* (1928), that constitutional protection against searches and seizures applied only when government conduct involved physical trespass onto a constitutionally protected space — "persons, houses, papers, and effects."[^1]

## Overview

Chief Justice Taft's *Olmstead* majority (277 U.S. 438)[^2] held that wiretaps placed on telephone wires outside Olmstead's property line did not constitute Fourth Amendment "searches" because the agents had not physically intruded on his "persons, houses, papers, and effects."[^3] The doctrine governed Fourth Amendment surveillance jurisprudence for 39 years until *Katz v. United States* (1967) abandoned it;[^4] the reasonable-expectation-of-privacy test associated with the case comes from Justice Harlan's concurrence.[^5]

The trespass doctrine left non-trespassory wiretapping constitutionally unregulated.[^6] The *Nardone v. United States* decisions (1937, 1939) applied §605 of the Federal Communications Act of 1934 to evidentiary use of intercepted communications;[^7] the Church Committee Book II electronic-surveillance history documents the 1940 presidential authorization and federal surveillance through 1968.[^8]

*United States v. Jones* (2012) partially revived a property-based analysis as an *alternative* path to Fourth Amendment protection (alongside the *Katz* framework) — but this revival is structurally narrow and does not restore the trespass doctrine as a *limitation* on protection.[^19]

## Historical significance

The trespass doctrine is the historical baseline for Fourth Amendment analysis of wiretapping and electronic surveillance.[^9] Its 39-year reign (1928–1967) coincided with the establishment of the postwar national-security state[^10] — including SHAMROCK (begun 1945)[^11], HTLINGUAL (begun 1952–53)[^12], and the early Counterintelligence Program (COINTELPRO) operations[^13] — meaning that decades of warrantless surveillance proceeded under a constitutional rule that explicitly tolerated non-trespassory wiretapping.[^14] *Katz*'s 1967 abandonment of the doctrine is the predicate for *Keith* (1972)[^15] and Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA) (1978).[^16]

## Open Questions

- **Question:** Given Book II’s account of warrantless wiretapping since the early 1930s and the 1940 presidential authorization requiring the Attorney General’s approval for each tap,[^17] what was the empirical scale of pre-*Katz* warrantless wiretapping under the trespass-doctrine permissive rule, particularly under Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) domestic-intelligence programs 1940–1967? **Consequence:** How would a documented operational baseline of that scale bear on the backdrop against which *Katz* (1967), *Keith* (1972), and FISA (1978) imposed warrant requirements?[^18]
