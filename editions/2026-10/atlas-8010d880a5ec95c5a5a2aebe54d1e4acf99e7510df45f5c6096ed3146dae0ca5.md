# "Invitation to Struggle"

Edition label: `2026-10`

**Definition:** The canonical mid-20th-century constitutional-theory formulation of the structural relationship between the President and Congress on foreign policy and national security, originating with <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Edward S. Corwin</span>'s *The President: Office and Powers* (1940; 5th rev. ed. 1984).[^1][^2] The formulation:

> "[T]he Constitution, considered only for its affirmative grants of powers capable of affecting the issue, is an invitation to struggle for the privilege of directing American foreign policy."[^3]

— Corwin et al., *The President: Office and Powers, 1787-1984* (5th rev. ed. 1984), p. 201.[^4]

## Overview

Corwin's formulation establishes that the U.S. constitutional architecture **does not pre-determine** which branch should have substantive authority over foreign policy and national security; instead, the architecture creates a structural incentive for inter-branch conflict that is resolved (when resolved at all) through political and institutional processes rather than through clear constitutional text.[^5] The "invitation to struggle" is descriptive — it does not prescribe an outcome, only a structural feature of the inter-branch relationship.

The formulation provides a constitutional-theory baseline for modern intelligence-oversight literature:

- [Bruemmer (1992)](https://openyls.law.yale.edu/entities/publication/f24d637a-5286-41ea-8701-e0ca63f4bc44) adopts Corwin's formulation as the framing for his argument that both President and Congress should decline the "invitation to struggle" and proceed by cooperative reorganization (President proposes; Congress approves up-or-down).[^6]
- <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Harold Koh, *The National Security Constitution* (1990)</span> offers "fast-track approval" as a model for foreign affairs legislation; according to Koh, the Iran-Contra scandal demonstrates that the "deeper systemic flaws" exposed by Vietnam and Watergate were never remedied.[^7]
- [Glennon, *Double Government* (2014)](https://harvardnsj.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2014/01/Glennon-Final.pdf) expressly invokes Corwin, arguing that the apparent balance among the branches is not the outcome of the struggle Corwin described: all three Madisonian institutions (the courts, Congress and the presidency) have lost effective authority to the Trumanite bureaucratic network.[^8]
- The temporal-evaluation problem in [Cormac et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0260210521000231) may be compared to an "invitation to struggle," but this is an analogy made here; Cormac et al. do not invoke Corwin or an interbranch-struggle framework.

## Analytical significance

One possible interpretation applies Corwin's formulation to the executive-counter-mobilization pattern described by Olmsted (1996) and Kitts (1996), with related evidence in McCormick & Smith (1987): when constitutional architecture invites struggle but supplies no decisive resolution mechanism, the executive branch's greater institutional resources, faster decision-making, and control of classified information may give it an advantage in contests over intelligence reform.[^9]

Bruemmer, a former Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) General Counsel writing in private practice and expressing his own views, recommends cooperative procedures using the Defense Base Closure model while favoring presidential leadership.[^10]
