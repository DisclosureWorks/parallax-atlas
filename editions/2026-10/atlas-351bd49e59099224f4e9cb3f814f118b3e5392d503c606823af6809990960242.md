# War Powers Resolution

Edition label: `2026-10`

**Definition:** The federal statute (P.L. 93-148; 87 Stat. 555; codified at 50 U.S.C. §§ 1541–1548) enacted November 7, 1973 over President Nixon's veto, intended to constrain presidential authority to commit U.S. armed forces to hostilities or imminent hostilities by requiring consultation with Congress, written reports, and termination of unauthorized commitments after 60–90 days.[^1]

## Overview

The Resolution emerged from the post-Vietnam congressional reassertion of war-powers authority.[^2] Its operative provisions:

- **Section 3 (consultation):** The President, in every possible instance, "shall consult with Congress" before introducing armed forces into hostilities and "shall consult regularly with the Congress" thereafter[^3]
- **Section 4 (reporting):** In the absence of a declaration of war, within 48 hours after armed forces are introduced into hostilities or into situations where imminent involvement in hostilities is clearly indicated by the circumstances, into the territory, airspace or waters of a foreign nation while equipped for combat (except deployments solely for supply, replacement, repair, or training), or in numbers which substantially enlarge United States Armed Forces equipped for combat already located in a foreign nation, the President shall submit a written report to the Speaker of the House and the President pro tem of the Senate[^4]
- **Section 5 (60-day clock):** Within 60 days after a report is submitted or is required to be submitted under Section 4(a)(1), whichever is earlier, the President shall terminate the use of the forces unless Congress declares war, enacts specific statutory authorization, extends the period by law, or is physically unable to meet as a result of an armed attack upon the United States; the period may be extended for no more than 30 days if the President certifies that unavoidable military necessity respecting the safety of U.S. forces requires their continued use while bringing about their prompt removal[^5]

Several presidents have viewed parts of the Resolution as unconstitutional, and presidents from Ford to Obama submitted more than 125 reports to Congress; with one exception, the reports characterized themselves as "consistent with," rather than pursuant to, its requirements.[^6] Presidents have generally maintained that they have authority independent of Congress to use force, although a 1980 Office of Legal Counsel (OLC) opinion accepted that Congress may impose the 60-day limit, and Obama administration officials said in 2011 that this opinion remained in effect (Congressional Research Service (CRS) notes it is not clear that other administrations agreed with it)[^7]; in the eight suits brought by Members of Congress through 2012 (as surveyed by CRS), no court ruled on the Resolution's constitutional validity.[^8]

The Resolution sits in a doctrinal cluster with Hughes-Ryan (covert-action notification), the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA, 1978), and the Intelligence Oversight Act (1980) — together comprising the post-Watergate / post-Vietnam congressional reassertion of authority over executive national-security conduct. The Cheney Iran-Contra Minority Report (1987) treats the 1970s view of congressional power behind this legislation as a break from constitutional practice[^9]; a 2002 OLC opinion states that the Office has questioned the Resolution's constitutionality on numerous occasions.[^10]

## Analytical Significance

The Resolution illustrates how congressional appropriations and statutory limits interact with judicial review. The Cheney Minority Report argues that Congress may not use its appropriations power to prevent the executive from fulfilling constitutionally mandated obligations; it expressly declines to make an argument about war powers.[^11] A *Curtiss-Wright* / *Youngstown* comparison places the Resolution in Jackson's Zone 3 because Congress acted to constrain the executive, but courts have generally declined to enforce it on standing, political-question, or mootness grounds.[^12]
